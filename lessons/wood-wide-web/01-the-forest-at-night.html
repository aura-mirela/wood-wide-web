Lesson 01 — The forest at night

Episode 1: Enter the forest. The environment is alive — subtle movement, moonlight, fog, fireflies. No interaction yet; the scene must earn attention on its own.

Concepts

  • Noise is the engine of everything procedural. Value noise / simplex noise gives you smooth randomness; fbm (fractal Brownian motion) stacks octaves of it for natural detail. You will reuse one noise.glsl include across the ground, the fog, the mushroom cap, and the mycelium. Read Book of Shaders ch. 11–13 before building.
  • Displacement. A flat PlaneGeometry with many segments + vertex shader moving position.y by fbm = terrain. The normal is now wrong; for a dark night scene you can fake lighting from height instead of recomputing normals.
  • Procedural trees without models. At night, silhouettes are enough: tapered cylinders (trunks) + stacked cones or billboarded blobs (canopy), placed by a seeded random function, scaled by noise. Distant trees can be a single ring of dark cardboard-cutout planes — nobody can tell at night.
  • Fog two ways. three.js built-in <fog>/<fogExp2> tints by camera distance (cheap, global). A custom ground-hugging fog is a big transparent plane whose fragment alpha = fbm drifting over time, faded by height. Use both: exp2 for depth, the plane for the creeping graveyard layer.
  • Fireflies. Your first Points + shader: gl_PointSize by distance, soft round sprite via distance(gl_PointCoord, vec2(0.5)), each point blinking on its own phase from a random per-point attribute.
  • CSS atmosphere. A vignette is one div with a radial-gradient overlay, pointer-events: none. Title text fades in with a CSS transition. The 3D scene and the DOM are two layers of the same composition.

Build steps

  1. src/shaders/includes/noise.glsl — take a known-good simplex/value noise implementation, add an fbm() on top. This file is sacred; everything imports it with #include.
  2. Ground: plane ~60×60 with 256+ segments, rotated flat, vertex displacement by fbm. Fragment: height-based color ramp from near-black soil to moonlit blue-grey. Add a uMoonDir uniform and brighten faces by dot(fakeNormal, uMoonDir) if you want to try normals from height (sample fbm at neighboring points — this is a great exercise).
  3. Moon: a circle or sphere far away, emissive white-blue; give it a halo with a slightly larger transparent plane whose alpha falls off radially.
  4. Trees: one <Trunk> component (cylinder, dark material), scatter 40–80 with seeded randomness (hash-based, not Math.random, so the forest is stable across reloads). Keep a clearing in the middle — the mushroom lives there later.
  5. Fog: <fogExp2 color="#0a0a16" density={0.045}> first. Then the low fog plane with drifting fbm alpha. Watch transparency sorting: render the fog plane last (renderOrder), depthWrite={false}.
  6. Fireflies: ~100 points in a box above the ground. Attributes: aPhase, aSpeed. Blink with smoothstep(0.9, 1.0, sin(uTime * aSpeed + aPhase)).
  7. DOM layer: vignette div + title ("The forest is alive — look around") that fades out after a few seconds or on first pointer move.

Exercises

  • Make wind: add uTime-scrolled noise to the firefly positions and a gentle sway to tree tops (bend by height² in the vertex shader).
  • Color-grade night properly: desaturate everything except the fireflies. (Preview of the Purkinje effect — human night vision loses color.)
  • Try react-responsive: on a narrow screen, lower segment counts and firefly count. This is your performance fallback pattern for the whole project.

Checkpoint

  • Why is fbm "noise stacked on noise", and what do octaves/lacunarity/gain do?
  • Why must the fog plane not write depth?
  • Why seeded randomness instead of Math.random() for placement?

Quiz

1. fbm gets its natural look by:

Octaves + lacunarity (frequency step) + gain (amplitude step).

2. Your displaced terrain looks flat-shaded wrong because:

Displacement happens in the vertex shader; normals must be recomputed or faked (e.g. from neighboring fbm samples).

3. The ground-fog plane shows hard rectangular seams where it intersects trees. Most likely fix:

Transparent surfaces writing depth clip what renders behind/through them.

4. Trees are placed with Math.random(). On every reload the forest changes. Why is a hash/seeded function better?

Stable worlds are debuggable and tunable; randomness you can't reproduce is a bug you can't reproduce.

5. Each firefly blinks on its own rhythm. The per-firefly phase should live in:

Constant per-point data is exactly what attributes are for; the GPU reads it with zero per-frame cost.
