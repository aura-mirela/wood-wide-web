Procedural track · camera position, look-at, fov, OrbitControls limits

Framing the camera so the forest feels like a landscape

What this lesson explains: how to choose where the camera stands, what it looks at, and how wide the lens is, then how to lock OrbitControls so a visitor can only turn and zoom inside that landscape. The ground in this project is a 20-unit square. Past its edge there is nothing. A landscape shot hides that edge, keeps the horizon quiet, and never puts the lens inside a tree.

Goal

After this lesson you should be able to:

  • read a shot off the camera-debug panel: position, look-at point, field of view, and the rotation line;
  • say what a rotation of [0, 0, 0] means, and when it is the correct result;
  • turn a chosen position and look-at point into an azimuth angle, a polar angle, and a distance;
  • set a short turn on the side that shows the edge of the ground, and a longer turn on the side that still looks like forest;
  • freeze up-and-down, and limit zoom so the camera cannot enter the foliage.

1. What “landscape” means in this scene

A landscape frame has three jobs:

  • You are standing in it. Camera height near y = 2 is eye level over hills whose amplitude is well under 1. A high y becomes a map. A y inside the ground becomes a burrow.
  • The square stays hidden. The ground is only 20 units across. If the turn or the zoom reveals the straight edge, the forest stops being a place and becomes a tile.
  • The lens stays outside the trees. Fir foliage is many flat cards crossing through each other. Inside that pile, the depth buffer swaps which card is in front from frame to frame. The branches blink. That blink is the camera being among the cards, not a broken rotation.

The shot this scene settled on is a camera at [0, 2, 12], looking at [-2, 1, -3.5], with fov: 50. The rest of the lesson is how those numbers were measured, and how the orbit limits were wrapped around them. If you move the camera, measure again. Do not treat 0.13 and 1.51 as constants that belong to every scene.

2. Hunt with the debug panel, then throw it away

CameraDebug does two visible things. An <axesHelper args={[10]} /> sits at the origin: red is X, green is Y, blue is Z, each line 10 units long. A Leva panel named Camera debug drives the camera every frame:

  • x, y, z — where the camera is. Each runs from -30 to 30.
  • lookX, lookY, lookZ — the world point the camera looks at. Same range. This is a point, not an angle.
  • fov — vertical field of view in degrees, from 20 to 75. The panel writes camera.fov and then calls updateProjectionMatrix(). Without that call the number changes and the picture does not.

The white line at the bottom left is camera.rotation after lookAt, in radians, to three decimal places. It is a readout. There is no rotation slider, so the line cannot fight the look-at point. Copy it when the frame is right, along with the six position and look-at numbers and fov.

While <CameraDebug /> is mounted, its useFrame overwrites the camera every frame. The position and fov on <Canvas> are not what you see. When the frame is chosen, delete src/debug/CameraDebug.jsx, remove <CameraDebug />, and put the numbers on <Canvas> and <OrbitControls>.

3. A rotation of zero can be the right answer

A three.js camera looks down its own −Z axis when rotation is [0, 0, 0]. If the camera and the look-at point share X and Y, and the look-at point is in front (its Z is smaller than the camera’s Z), lookAt leaves the rotation at zero.

Example: position [0, 2, 13], look-at [0, 2, -3.5]. The direction is straight along −Z. The readout [0.000, 0.000, 0.000] is that aim, not a stuck label. lookZ only picks a point on that line. Any look-at point with the same X, the same Y, and a Z in front of the camera produces the same rotation.

The kept shot is not that case. The camera is at [0, 2, 12] and the target is [-2, 1, -3.5], so the aim is a little to the side and a little downward. The Euler line will not be all zeros. You still do not type that Euler line into OrbitControls. The controls want a target, then angles around that target.

4. Position and lens go on the canvas. The aim goes on the controls.

<Canvas camera={{ position: [0, 2, 12], fov: 50 }}>
  <OrbitControls target={[-2, 1, -3.5]} />
</Canvas>
  • position is the eye.
  • fov is the vertical lens angle in degrees. 50 is a moderate lens: tighter than React Three Fiber’s default 75, close to three.js’s default 50. Wider lenses show more of the square’s edge. Narrower lenses feel like a telescope.
  • target is the look-at point. OrbitControls orbits around it. If you omit target, the default is the origin, and the first frame tilts the camera down toward (0, 0, 0).

Scroll-wheel zoom does not change fov. It moves the camera closer to or farther from target. The lens stays 50. The travel is limited with distance, in the next sections.

5. Measure azimuth, polar angle, and distance from the two points

Take the vector from the target back to the camera:

offset = camera.position − target
       = (0 − (−2),  2 − 1,  12 − (−3.5))
       = (2, 1, 15.5)

distance = length(offset) ≈ 15.7
azimuth  = atan2(offset.x, offset.z) = atan2(2, 15.5) ≈ 0.13
polar    = acos(offset.y / distance) = acos(1 / 15.7) ≈ 1.51

All three results are in radians, except distance, which is in scene units.

  • Azimuth is the left–right angle around the green Y axis. 0 means the camera sits on the target’s +Z side. This shot is about 0.13 radians (roughly 7°) off that.
  • Polar is the nod, measured down from straight above the target. 0 is overhead. π/2 (about 1.57) is level with the target. 1.51 is a few degrees above level, so the lens looks slightly down and the ground sits in the lower frame. That slight downward look is a lot of what makes the view feel like a place you are standing in.
  • Distance is how far the eye is from the target along that same line. About 15.7 here.

Recompute these three whenever you change position or target. The limits in the next section are offsets around this measurement.

6. A short turn toward the edge, a long turn into the woods

Left and right do not have to share one number. Looking left decreases the azimuth and stops at minAzimuthAngle. Looking right increases it and stops at maxAzimuthAngle.

minAzimuthAngle={0.13 - 0.2}
maxAzimuthAngle={0.13 + 0.7}

0.13 is the measured azimuth, so the window is centered on the shot you liked. 0.2 radians is about 11° of turn to the left. 0.7 radians is about 40° to the right. The left stop is the short one because that direction reveals the end of the 20-unit plane. The right stop is the long one because that direction still reads as forest.

Shrink the small number if the edge still enters the frame. Grow the large number if the wooded side can show more before it should stop. Swap them if you discover the edge is on the other side.

Up and down stay fixed by giving both polar limits the measured polar angle:

minPolarAngle={1.51}
maxPolarAngle={1.51}

Equal values freeze the nod. The visitor can turn their head, not tip it. OrbitControls never rolls around Z, so there is no third limit to set. rotation.z stays 0.

7. Zoom is a distance from the target

minDistance={12}
maxDistance={16}

These are scene units from target, along the orbit line. The framed distance is about 15.7, so 12…16 is a small dolly around that shot. 12 is as close as the scroll wheel may come. 16 is as far as it may go.

minDistance is what keeps the lens out of the firs. It is the distance to the target, not the distance to the nearest leaf. A crown that sits between the eye and the target can still surround the camera if the minimum is too small. At 6, the path runs through foliage and the leaf cards blink. Raise the minimum until the branches stay in front of the lens. Lowering camera.near does not fix that blink. The near plane only clips fragments closer than 0.1. The flicker is two foliage cards fighting for the same depth.

maxDistance is the other landscape guard. Pull back too far and the square’s boundary becomes the subject. 16 stops just past the framed shot.

Build steps (from this codebase)

  1. Comment out <OrbitControls /> and mount <CameraDebug />. Confirm the axes: red X, green Y, blue Z.
  2. Move x y z, the look-at point, and fov until the ground fills the lower frame and the far side still looks like forest. Write down all seven numbers and the rotation line.
  3. Remove the debug component. Put position and fov on <Canvas camera>. Put the look-at point on <OrbitControls target>.
  4. From those two points, compute distance, atan2(offset.x, offset.z), and acos(offset.y / distance).
  5. Center the azimuth window on that azimuth. Use a smaller allowance on the side that shows the plane edge.
  6. Set minPolarAngle and maxPolarAngle to the same polar angle.
  7. Set minDistance and maxDistance around the measured distance, wide enough to breathe and tight enough that you cannot enter a crown or reveal the square.

Exercises

  1. With the debug panel on, aim the camera straight down −Z (same X, same Y, look-at Z in front). Predict the rotation line before you look. It should read about [0.000, 0.000, 0.000].
  2. Restore the landscape shot. On paper, compute offset, distance, azimuth, and polar angle from [0, 2, 12] and [-2, 1, -3.5]. Compare them to the limits in Experience.jsx.
  3. Change only the left allowance from 0.2 to 0.8, orbit left, and watch for the edge of the plane. Put 0.2 back.
  4. Set minPolarAngle to 1.51 - 0.15 and maxPolarAngle to 1.51 + 0.15. The nod should return. Set both back to 1.51 and confirm up and down are fixed.
  5. Set minDistance to 6 and dolly into a fir until the branches blink. Write one sentence: which surfaces are fighting, and why raising minDistance stops it. Restore 12.

Checkpoint

  • Which Leva rows are the camera’s position, which are the look-at point, and which control is the lens?
  • Why can the rotation line be all zeros while lookZ is not zero?
  • What does OrbitControls orbit around if you forget target?
  • Which prop stops a left turn, and which number in 0.13 - 0.2 is the starting angle rather than the allowance?
  • Why are minPolarAngle and maxPolarAngle equal, and why is there no Z-rotation limit?
  • Is minDistance a distance to the nearest tree, or a distance to the orbit target?

Quiz

1. The rotation line at the bottom left is:

The panel writes position and calls lookAt. The line only reports the Euler angles that aim produced.

2. Position [0, 2, 13] and look-at [0, 2, -3.5] produce rotation [0, 0, 0] because:

Same X, same Y, target in front: the direction is −Z. lookAt leaves the identity rotation.

3. For this shot, 0.13 and 1.51 are:

atan2(2, 15.5) ≈ 0.13 and acos(1 / 15.7) ≈ 1.51. Move the camera and these numbers change.

4. minAzimuthAngle={0.13 - 0.2} and maxAzimuthAngle={0.13 + 0.7} mean:

Looking left decreases azimuth toward the minimum. The short side is the one that shows the edge of the plane.

5. Setting both polar angles to 1.51:

Polar angle is the nod. Equal min and max lock it. Orbit controls do not roll, so Z has no matching prop.

6. The branches blink when the camera is very near because:

minDistance pulls the eye back from the target so the path no longer runs through the crown. It is not a distance to each tree.
